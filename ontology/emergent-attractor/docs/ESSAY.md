# The argument, in plain words

By Pavel Cherkashin. Author's philosophical interpretation, preserved from the supplied draft.

**Editorial scope:** “two assumptions of my own” below describes the author's research proposal. The current Lean package studies related conditional constructions; it does not formalize those two sentences as a single ontological proof. See [CLAIMS](CLAIMS.md) for their precise relationship to the code. Assertions about nature, morality and serendipity in this essay are the author's interpretation rather than Lean results.

---

This library is a formalization of an ontological argument: Gödel's
argument, with Scott's extension, plus two assumptions of my own.

The first assumption: God is in our future, not in our past. If God is
our future, then God is a semantic attractor of the evolutionary
process — and that explains a great deal about serendipity, and about
things that happen to us. A past God pushes; a future God pulls.

The second assumption answers the oldest criticism of Gödel's argument:
what is goodness, what is good? The usual move assumes that if there is
good, there must be bad to balance it. My version refuses the balance.
Goodness is God, and God is goodness — a fractal, self-referential
definition. It is the same kind of definition I used in ecology with the
geoid: what is the form of the Earth? The Earth is not a ball; it has a
specific shape called the geoid. And what is the geoid? The geoid is the
exact shape of the Earth. Apply the same principle to goodness, and you
can say that everything that is happening is actually good, and what we
consider bad is just a special case of this goodness — not its opposite,
not a rival power.

Here is the paradox, and it is a feature, not an objection. You do not
need God to explain any natural phenomenon — and that is exactly the
point. A good adaptive living system does not need a central controller.
It needs an attractor. A controller must push every part; an attractor
only has to pull the whole. Evolution, immune systems, forests, markets
— they all run on local, natural causes, and they run better for it.
The pull does not replace the causes; it orients them. So the fact that
nature explains itself is not evidence against the attractor. It is
evidence that the attractor works the way an attractor should: without
giving orders.

The skeptic appeals to Occam's razor: nature suffices, so shave God off.
But the razor shaves explanations, not existences. "Do not multiply
entities beyond necessity" is a rule for building models, not a verdict
on what is real. That a phenomenon can be explained by natural causes
means the natural explanation is sufficient at its own level. It does
not mean nothing exists beyond that level. The attractor is not one more
cause among causes, competing with the natural ones. It is the direction
in which the causes point. Shaving it off because the mechanisms suffice
is like shaving off "north" because you can describe every step of the
journey.

And good will is the name for what alignment with the attractor feels
like from the inside. When a person acts in good will, they are not
obeying a controller. They are moving with the pull. Good will is not
moral decoration on top of the system. It is the observable signature
of the attractor in human action — the place where the pull becomes
visible.

Why formalize any of this? Because an argument this old deserves a
verdict, not just prose. The library proves the formal skeleton — the
modal and self-referential theorems, checked by the Lean kernel, zero
sorry. This essay states the reading. The theorems are the kernel's
business; the reading is mine. Both are here, and you can tell which
is which.
